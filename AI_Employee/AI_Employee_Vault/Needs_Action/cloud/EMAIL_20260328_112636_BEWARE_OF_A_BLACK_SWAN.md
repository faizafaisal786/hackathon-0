A “black swan” is a rare and devastating event that shatters assumptions. The term, popularized by Nassim Nicholas Taleb, describes an occurrence that lies outside normal expectations, carries extreme impact, and is rationalized only after it happens. Before it arrives, experts dismiss it. After it arrives, everyone claims it was inevitable. Black swans are not simply crises. They are system-breaking moments — the kind that alter history’s direction in a matter of hours. On April 6, 1994, one such moment unfolded over the skies of Kigali, Rwanda. A plane carrying President Juvénal Habyarimana of Rwanda and President Cyprien Ntaryamira of Burundi was struck by a missile as it approached the airport. Within minutes, the aircraft fell from the sky. In that instant, the fragile architecture of peace collapsed. Within hours, roadblocks went up. Within days, killing spread. Within weeks, the machinery of genocide moved with terrifying speed. In one hundred days, more than 800,000 lives were extinguished. A regime collapsed. A nation was consumed.
